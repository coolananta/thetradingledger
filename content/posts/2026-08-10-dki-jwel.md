## DKI: a Hong Kong mobile-gaming name and a VWAP level that was already tired

<span class="pill pill-china">DKI · Hong Kong</span> <span class="pill pill-hold">0.373M Float</span>

DKI is Darkiris Inc., a Hong Kong-based mobile game developer trading on Nasdaq under the Electronic Gaming & Multimedia industry, Communication Services sector. It's a genuinely thin name — a **0.373 million share float** — and one that's leaned into an AI narrative recently, including the commercial launch of a generative AI video platform alongside its core mobile gaming business. That AI angle is part of what fueled the retail momentum behind today's move.

My first trade went in while price was sitting just below a **4-month high**. The reasoning, laid out honestly:

**Macro:** an extremely low float, a Hong Kong listing — a geography that, in my own tracked sample of big movers, has produced the highest average return percentage of anywhere I follow — and a catalyst combining retail momentum with an AI narrative.

**Technical:** price sitting below a multi-month high, a large initial impulse leg of roughly **$6.58**, and — this is the detail that matters most in hindsight — **a fourth tap of VWAP**.

{{< callout type="important" title="I already knew this rule. I just didn't apply it here." >}}
Four sessions ago, writing about YXT, I put this exact principle into words: a level that gets tapped in tight succession isn't being respected, it's being chewed through. Three taps was already the threshold I'd identified as a warning sign. DKI gave me a **fourth**. It turns out that instinct has real backing beyond my own notes — one VWAP-focused trading guide I came across afterward puts it almost identically: *a reclaim into a falling VWAP is fighting the tape, and a fourth touch of a flat VWAP is closer to a coin flip than a setup.* There's also a separate, sharper point worth sitting with: VWAP as an indicator is built on volume, and it's most reliable on liquid names with real two-sided participation. On a float this thin, the line itself is a weaker signal to begin with — a fourth tap on an illiquid 0.373M-float stock is arguably a coin flip stacked on top of a shakier foundation than a fourth tap on a liquid large-cap would be.
{{< /callout >}}

I want to give credit where it's due: once I was in a losing position, I executed the exit well. Reading Level 2 and honoring my mental stop instead of hoping is a real skill, and I don't think today's DKI trade reflects badly on that part of my process. What I do dislike is separate from the read itself — I entered and exited the same name **three separate times**, six fills total, on a stock that had already told me once that the setup wasn't there. The first exit should have ended my interest in DKI for the morning. It didn't.

## JWEL: a panic trade taken at the wrong altitude

<span class="pill pill-china">JWEL · China</span> <span class="pill pill-hold">2.03M Float</span>

JWEL is Jowell Global Ltd., a Shanghai-based e-commerce company selling cosmetics, health and nutritional supplements, and household products through its own platform and roughly 26,000 Love Home retail stores across China. It's a stock with real scar tissue — it IPO'd at $139.36 back in 2021 and has since fallen over 98% — and one that Nasdaq has flagged for minimum bid price compliance before, leading to a prior 1-for-16 share consolidation. Today's move had no clean company-specific catalyst behind it; this is squarely what one financial write-up called, almost describing this exact stock, a move "more consistent with the dynamics of a thinly traded micro-cap than a clearly identifiable change in fundamentals."

I took this trade because price had tapped the **9-EMA on the hourly chart twice** and bounced strongly both times — a real, legitimate signal on its own. Where it went wrong was the entry I actually took versus the one that was available:

Buying directly into a rejection at a three-year high isn't the same trade as buying a confirmed 3-minute EMA support level, even though both setups involve the same stock and the same larger pattern. The $7 this cost me is almost beside the point — the real lesson is that I shouldn't have taken a position at all until price actually emerged above that 3-year high following a genuine panic candle.

{{< callout type="tip" title="A rule I already hold, restated for myself" >}}
I love panic trades — but only when they happen on a strong trend, above the major highs, not into them. JWEL was the second setup of the morning, and I don't think it's a coincidence that both mistakes today involved acting a half-step early relative to a rule I already had.

## The actual lesson isn't about either ticker

Both of today's losses trace back to the same root cause, and it isn't "wrong stock" or "wrong setup." It's that I opened a brand-new session — one I'd never traded before this morning — and immediately applied full size and full conviction to it, instead of treating it the way I'd treat any other unproven edge: small, deliberate, and closely tracked before scaling up. The rules I broke today (respecting VWAP tap counts, waiting for confirmation instead of chasing a rejection) are rules I already follow reasonably well during regular hours. They just hadn't been stress-tested in the pre-market yet, and this morning was that test.

Extended-hours data was a genuinely good discovery. The mistake wasn't opening that door — it was walking through it at full speed on day one.
