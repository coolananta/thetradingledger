This is the account of both mistakes, on YXT and on the one I didn't even touch, INLF.

## The Trade I Shouldn't Have Taken

<span class="pill pill-china">YXT · NASDAQ</span> <span class="pill pill-sell">Overtraded</span>

YXT.com Group Holding is a Suzhou-based enterprise software company — corporate learning platforms, e-learning systems, and an AI-augmented productivity push it markets internationally under the Radnova name. It's a familiar shape in this sector: small float, a share price that spent time under a dollar, a compliance letter from Nasdaq for failing the minimum bid price rule, and a fix through consolidating the share count. The company cleared that compliance issue days before this session. That kind of housekeeping tends to draw a very specific type of trader back to a name — the kind watching for the next catalyst to arrive on a cleaner structure.

Today, it arrived.

### Where it went wrong

At the open, YXT tapped VWAP for the **third time** in close succession. I took the trade anyway.

{{< callout type="important" title="The setup wasn't broken. The timing was." >}}
VWAP reclaim-and-bounce is a real, working setup — but it needs room between the taps. Three touches in tight succession is the market chewing through the level, not respecting it. By the time I entered, price had only barely poked above VWAP while the 12-EMA on the 3-minute chart — the average that had been dictating direction all morning — was still pointed down and still very much under price. That single reading should have kept me out entirely.
{{< /callout >}}

The stock was already selling off in earnest by the time my fill went through. Over $50 of today's loss lives inside that one decision.

## The Trade I Should Have Taken

A few hours later, on the daily chart, YXT did something it had never done before: it traded above its **200-day moving average** for the first time. That alone is a signal worth respecting. What made it a real signal, and not a fluke poke through the line, was what happened next — price came back down and **double-bottomed at $6.52**, right at that same level, before turning back up.

That was the trade. I didn't take it.

Premarket volume alone ran to roughly **2.8 times the entire float** before the opening bell — a name this thin doesn't move that kind of size without real intent behind it. AI-transformation coverage and the fresh Nasdaq compliance news gave the crowd a reason to show up early, and it did.

Beyond the 200 DMA, YXT broke its **52-week high of $11.36** after an initial tap of the 9-EMA on the 30-minute chart near $6.50 and a second, confirming tap of that same average later on. That double tap was good for a further **173.59% surge off the 52-week high** — a level that, once cleared, stopped acting like resistance and started acting like a floor.

The session high of $32.16 lines up with two independent things at once: it's within a hair of the psychological 1000%-from-base level, and it sits almost exactly on the **1.618 Fibonacci extension** of the impulse leg from $2.69 to $20.79 — a projected target of roughly **$31.98**. Two unrelated methods of measuring the move, landing on the same number, at a level that had already rejected price three separate times on the daily chart. That kind of confluence doesn't happen by accident often enough to ignore it.

## The One I Left Alone: INLF

<span class="pill pill-china">INLF · NASDAQ</span> <span class="pill pill-skip">Correctly Skipped</span>

INLIF Limited is a different kind of company entirely — a Quanzhou-based manufacturer of manipulator arms for injection molding machinery, more industrial robotics than software. It's also no stranger to reverse share combinations in pursuit of Nasdaq's minimum bid price rule; this is a company that has restructured its share count more than once this year alone to stay listed. That history matters context-wise: a stock with that kind of share-count engineering behind it tends to trade with outsized, mechanical volatility once real volume shows up.

Price ran from $3.88 up to $8.61, straight into daily resistance at $8.21 — got rejected, and sold off hard. That's the point most traders would have walked away entirely. Instead, price held **VWAP together with the 12-EMA on the 15-minute chart for 45 minutes**, sitting around the $6 level without giving that ground back. That's a real tell: sellers had their shot after the resistance rejection and still couldn't push it lower. From there it propelled up to $9.44, roughly 200% off the $3.16 base.

{{< callout type="tip" title="Skipping this one wasn't a mistake — the reasoning matters more than the outcome" >}}
INLF was choppy the entire way, and it never broke a level significant enough to justify holding it for size or for long. The right read on a stock like this is to treat any position as a quick, defined scalp — take profit early, don't get greedy waiting for a level that isn't there. Missing the size of the move on INLF stings less than the YXT double-bottom, because the decision *not* to commit real size to a choppy tape was the correct one, even in hindsight.
{{< /callout >}}

## What Actually Happened Today, Underneath the P&L

Two stocks. One printed over 1000%. The other printed 200% out of a level that never should have held and did anyway. I was positioned wrong on the first one at the open, right about the setup on the second attempt and unwilling to act on it, and correctly cautious — for the right reasons — on the third.

The $88.85 loss isn't really a story about VWAP or 200-day moving averages. It's a story about trading a fading setup for the fourth time when it had already told me three times it was done, and then not trusting the one signal that actually mattered because I was still carrying the last one. The chart resets every session. Whether I do is the only variable that was actually in my control today.
