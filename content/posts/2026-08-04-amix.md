## Abstract

I sat through most of the session doing nothing. No setups, no signal, just watching tape that refused to move. Then, in the back half of the day, one stock did what a 0.526-million-share float stock does when 120 million shares trade through it: it detonated. I was in it. I was out of it. I closed the day up four dollars on a stock that handed out a legitimate 500%+ move. This is my attempt to reconstruct exactly what happened, with numbers, not just memory — because memory is exactly the thing hesitation likes to hide inside.

## The Instrument

AMIX is a Healthcare-sector name, specifically Medical Devices — a **0.526 million share float**, thin enough that ordinary volume can move it and extraordinary volume can turn it into something closer to a controlled detonation than a stock chart.

The catalyst was concrete and specific: the announcement of **US Patent No. 12,433,670**, covering precision neuromodulation for nerve sensing, mapping, and targeted cancer and organ-function therapies. Not a vague partnership, not a rumor — an issued patent, which is about as unambiguous as a catalyst gets in this sector.

The float rotated roughly **228 times over** during the session. Before the bell even rang, premarket volume alone had already turned the float over **more than four times**. That second number is the one I should have weighted more heavily in real time — a float rotating four-plus times before regular hours even begin is not noise. It's a crowd already forming outside a door that hasn't opened yet.

## The Chart Mechanics

The move that eventually pulled the stock from a $3.49 base to a $21.67 high didn't arrive as a single clean breakout. It built in stages, and each stage had its own signature.

**Stage one — the impulse leg.** From a base of $3.50, the stock put in an initial impulse move of **100%** — a clean double. This is the leg that put the name on my radar in the first place.

**Stage two — the flush.** From the impulse leg's high, price cut back **38%**. This is the point at which I disengaged mentally from the setup. A 38% retracement of an impulse leg is steep, and on a lower timeframe it reads as chop, possibly as failure. I read it that way. I decided the momentum wasn't strong enough and moved on.

**Stage three — the reclaim.** The stock built a **triple bounce off the 9-EMA on the 30-minute chart** — three separate tests of the same moving average, three separate holds. That's not a coincidence pattern; on a 30-minute chart, a level that gets defended three times in succession is a level the tape has decided to respect, at least for now. Shortly after, price broke out through the **100-day moving average at $7.54**, on **1-minute volume of 97.54K** — meaningfully above what that stock does on an average 1-minute bar. Volume confirming a moving-average break is close to the textbook definition of a valid breakout.

The retracement took the stock from roughly $7.00 back down toward the mid-$4 range before the reclaim and the 100-DMA breakout at $7.54 put it back on offer. I saw the flush. I underweighted the reclaim.

## The Trade I Took

I took **100 shares at $6.28 at 10:49**, inside the reclaim, ahead of the confirmed 100-DMA breakout. On its face, that's not a bad entry — it's earlier than the breakout print, which usually means better price. What undid the trade wasn't the entry. It was the exit.

I liquidated the position **out of nothing other than vacillation.** Not a stop being hit. Not a technical level failing. Indecision, acted on as if it were a signal.

The honest correction here, looking at it after the fact: the setup that got me in was a 30-minute pattern — a triple EMA bounce, a higher-timeframe structure. I exited based on noise from a much lower timeframe, without ever stepping back up to the chart that had actually justified the trade in the first place. If I had checked the 30-minute chart before closing the position, I very likely hold it longer. The mismatch between the timeframe I used to enter and the timeframe I used to exit is, on its own, close to a complete explanation for why this trade returned four dollars instead of what it should have.

## The Level I Left on the Table

This is the part that's hardest to write plainly, so I'll just write it plainly.

At **$10.54** — a strong psychological level in its own right — price put in a **double bottom on the 50-EMA on the 1-minute chart**, which at that exact moment was also sitting at the **9-EMA on the 15-minute chart**. Two moving averages, from two different timeframes, converging on the same price, at the same level, at the same time.

An entry at that confluence level to the eventual session high represents a return of roughly **106%** — essentially the "100% on my capital" figure that was sitting there, in real time, for anyone willing to act on a two-timeframe support confluence at a round psychological number, on a stock that had already proven it had the volume and the catalyst to keep going.

I felt the move. I had, in my own words, a *trained gut instinct* telling me the stock had room to run toward the 200-day moving average at $22.46 — a level the session high of $21.67 ultimately came within roughly 3.5% of. The instinct was correct. It simply never got translated into an order.

## Extension in Context

A 521.54% move from a $3.49 base is not a number that shows up often, and it's worth putting some scale around it rather than just stating it.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Base price | $3.49 |
| Session high | $21.67 |
| % move from base | 521.54% |
| Float | 0.526M shares |
| Total volume | 120M shares |
| Float rotation (total) | ≈228x |
| Premarket volume | 2.17M shares |
| Float rotation (premarket) | ≈4.13x |
| 100 DMA breakout level | $7.54 |
| 200 DMA (unreached) | $22.46 |

A float this small, rotating over two hundred times in a single session, on a catalyst with genuine specificity behind it, is close to the ideal condition set for exactly the kind of move this stock produced. None of the pieces here were hidden. The float was known before the open. The premarket volume was known before the open. The patent announcement was known before the open. What wasn't fixed in advance was whether I would trust the setup enough to size and hold a position through it.

## The Actual Failure Point

It would be convenient to say the failure was a missed entry at $10.54. It wasn't, not really. Missing one entry on a fast-moving stock is normal — nobody catches every level. The real failure sits earlier and is more structural: **a documented, correctly-identified higher-timeframe setup (the 30-minute triple EMA bounce) was overridden by an undocumented, lower-timeframe feeling** at the moment of exit. The system worked right up until the point where I stopped using it.

That's a different problem than "I don't have good setups" or "I can't read a chart." Both of those were fine today. The gap was between recognizing a valid pattern and actually staying inside the trade that pattern justified.

## What I'm Taking From This

- A float rotating multiple times over in the premarket session, before the open even happens, is itself a signal worth acting on with more conviction — not just a number to note in passing.
- A retracement of an impulse leg, even a steep one, is not automatically a failure of the setup. The 30-minute triple EMA bounce that followed the 38% flush was the actual tell, and it printed in plain view.
- Exit decisions need to be made on the same timeframe — or higher — than the one that justified the entry. Dropping to a lower timeframe to make an exit decision is how a good trade gets cut short for no defensible reason.
- Multi-timeframe confluence — the same price level showing up as support on two different EMAs across two different timeframes — is a stronger signal than either one alone, and deserves to be treated as such in the moment, not just recognized afterward.
- A trained instinct that correctly anticipates a level (the pull toward the 200 DMA, in this case) is only useful if it's converted into a position. Recognizing the move in advance and not acting on it produces the exact same P&L as not recognizing it at all.

Four dollars is the number on the account today. It isn't the number that mattered. The number that mattered was the gap between what the chart was showing and what I was willing to trust while it was happening — and that gap is the only part of today actually worth carrying forward.
