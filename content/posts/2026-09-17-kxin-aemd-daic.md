## KXIN: a trade I can't fully explain, even to myself

<span class="pill pill-china">KXIN · China</span> <span class="pill pill-hold">1.51M Float</span>

Kaixin Holdings is a Cayman Islands-incorporated automotive retail company running its car sales business through its Hong Kong subsidiary, Jet Sound Hong Kong. The real number behind today's move: first-half 2026 revenue of **$667,000**, up **602% year-over-year** from $95,000, disclosed in a Form 6-K. On the surface that's an exciting headline. Underneath it, the picture is a lot less clean — gross profit for the same period actually **fell 76% to just $23,000**, the company disclosed **substantial doubt about its ability to continue as a going concern** (citing $9.3 million in current liabilities against $1.6 million in current assets), and a separate escrow arrangement tied to Kaixin's August acquisition of a Hong Kong auto wholesaler means **10 million additional Class A shares** are already earmarked for issuance — real, near-term dilution risk stacked directly on top of a revenue number that's still tiny in absolute terms.

None of that stopped the stock from moving. But it's exactly why the move never extended: the only thing on the technical side pointing higher was a **double tap of the 9-EMA on the 30-minute chart** — no fresh confirmation, no clean base, nothing beyond one moving-average bounce fighting against a real list of fundamental red flags.

I took the trade anyway. 100 shares at 6:32 AM. Lost **$18**. Looking back at it honestly, I don't have a good answer for why I sized into this one — the technical case was thin, and the fundamental case was actively bad. That combination should have been a pass, not a position.

## AEMD: a real $180M deal, a single one-minute candle, and a lucky trade I won't take credit for

<span class="pill pill-hold">1.58M Float</span> <span class="pill pill-slate">Medical Devices</span>

Aethlon Medical — developer of the Hemopurifier, a clinical-stage device designed to filter cancer-promoting exosomes and dangerous viruses from the bloodstream — announced a definitive all-stock merger with North Immunology at 6:35 AM. This is about as real a catalyst as small-cap biotech produces: North Immunology is developing NOR-101, a bispecific antibody aimed at atopic dermatitis, positioned as a direct challenger to Sanofi's blockbuster Dupixent. The deal comes with a **$180 million private placement**, and once it closes the combined company becomes **North Immunology, Inc.**, trading under the new ticker **NRTX**. Current AEMD holders keep roughly **4.75%** of the combined entity, plus contingent value rights tied to the legacy Hemopurifier business.

The market's reaction was almost violent. Independent reporting shows AEMD surging over **400%** in pre-market trading. My own read of the tape: the stock opened at **$4.18** and printed a high of **$14 within a single one-minute candle** before closing that same candle back down at **$5.80** — a base-to-high move of **879.67%** measured against the $1.43 starting point, all inside thirteen minutes. A short interest of **29.22%** on a 1.58 million share float explains a huge part of why this got so violent: short sellers caught in a real, deal-driven gap up become forced buyers at exactly the moment they least want to be, and that unwind can be just as sharp on the way back down as it was on the way up.

I didn't touch that first spike — correctly, since a move that size giving back almost half its gains typically keeps giving it back rather than reclaiming the highs, and it did exactly that after double-tapping the psychological $10 level on the way down.

Where I actually traded was later: price spent from **8:06 to 9:05 AM** running cleanly along the **50-EMA on the 1-minute chart**. My rule, stated plainly, is to enter near support, not after a fresh impulse away from it. I broke that rule — entering roughly 50 cents above my own preferred level at 9:06 AM with 50 shares, for a **$14 gain**. I'm not proud of this one even though it made money, because the reasoning behind it was closer to impulse than process. Price actually came back down to the 50-EMA level at 9:10 AM, four minutes later — the entry I should have taken, and one that would have been worth roughly **$100** instead of $14. Live trading doesn't hand you the version of the chart you can only see afterward, but the gap between what I did and what my own stated rules call for here is worth sitting with.

## DAIC: the one that was already on my list

<span class="pill pill-hold">1.34M Float</span> <span class="pill pill-slate">Technology · IT Services</span>

This is the one that actually stings, because I didn't discover DAIC in the moment — it was already on my watchlist before the move happened.

CID HoldCo, operating as **Dot Ai**, is an IoT and AI-based SaaS company building asset-tracking and workflow software for industrial operations, headquartered in Las Vegas. At 4:05 AM, finviz reported that the company had signed a **legally binding** Summary of Principal Terms to acquire 100% of **Envoy Technologies**, a Los Angeles electric-vehicle-sharing operator, from BladeRanger Ltd. (an Israeli-listed robotics company) and Blink Charging Co. This is a real, substantive deal: $65 million in equity consideration at $6.00 per share, roughly 10.8 million shares issued at closing, representing about **67.3% of the post-closing company** — Envoy's side effectively taking control of the combined entity. The same week, CID HoldCo also settled a senior default claim, converting over $1 million in debt into equity to clear its liens. The acquisition is targeted to close by October 6th, with definitive agreements expected by September 25th — meaningfully more advanced than a loose non-binding letter of intent.

### Why DAIC's all-time high on the chart isn't actually real

Before getting to the setup itself, there's something worth flagging that directly matters for reading this chart correctly: DAIC's listed all-time high shows **$1,875.00**, dated to its very first trading day after a SPAC merger in June 2025. That number is essentially fiction. DAIC executed a **1-for-25 reverse stock split** effective May 29, 2026, after its share price fell below Nasdaq's $1.00 minimum bid requirement. Chart platforms typically apply reverse splits retroactively across the entire price history — so a real historical print of **$75** on day one becomes **$75 × 25 = $1,875** once the adjustment is applied backward. Nothing about that reflects real trading activity at that inflated level; it's the same single print, just relabeled in today's share-count terms. This is exactly the kind of thing worth checking before trusting any "all-time high" or "past big move" figure a charting platform hands you.

The real, honestly-adjusted sequence looks like this:

| Date | Event | Real Organic Move? |
|---|---|---|
| Jun 23, 2025 | Nasdaq debut via SPAC merger with ShoulderUp Technology Acquisition Corp. | Yes — but a single volatile debut print, not a repeatable pattern |
| May 29, 2026 | 1-for-25 reverse stock split (Nasdaq minimum-bid compliance) | **No** — mechanical share-count adjustment, zero change in value |
| Aug 19, 2026 | Stock sets a new all-time low of $0.37, post-split | Yes — real, continued organic decline |
| Sep 17, 2026 (today) | Binding term sheet to acquire Envoy Technologies + debt settlement | **Yes** — real catalyst, shares ran from a $2.01 close to as high as $7.14 intraday |

One trader community note I came across while checking this history described DAIC plainly as having "a history of supernovas" — thin float, frequent capital raises, and a pattern of sharp, catalyst-driven spikes between long stretches of decay. That reputation tracks with everything above: a stock that burns most of its value between events, then rips violently higher the moment a real one arrives.

None of these three names is a coincidence sitting next to the others. All three carried real short interest north of 25%, all three had genuine corporate catalysts (not rumors), and all three come from sectors — Technology, Consumer Cyclical, and Healthcare — that keep showing up as the biggest movers in my own tracked data. Short interest this heavy is exactly the double-edged setup it always is: it forces sellers into becoming unwilling buyers on the way up, and it can just as easily unwind into an equally sharp sell-off once the squeeze runs its course, the way AEMD's did after tagging $10.

## What today is actually about

I don't fail at finding these setups. Three real, catalyst-backed, heavily-shorted names in a single session, two of which I correctly flagged as legitimate before or during the move — that's not a research problem. The problem is narrower and more specific than that: a bad habit of dismissing my own watchlist names without giving them a full, deliberate look before the open, and a tendency to enter trades a few minutes and a few cents away from the level my own rules actually call for. KXIN should have been a pass entirely. AEMD should have been a patient entry at 9:10, not an impulsive one at 9:06. DAIC should have been the easiest trade of the entire week, sitting on a list I'd already built myself.

Going forward, the sectors worth staying locked onto haven't changed: **Technology, Healthcare, Basic Materials, Consumer Cyclical, and Consumer Defensive** keep producing the names that actually move. The setups are findable. Today proved that three times over. What isn't solved yet is making sure a stock earning a spot on my own watchlist actually gets the full analysis that earned it that spot in the first place.
