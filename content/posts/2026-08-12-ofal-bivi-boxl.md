Twenty-two fills across two stocks in under three hours, and I still ended the day green. I want to sit with that contradiction for a second before explaining it away, because the honest version of today isn't "I traded well." It's "I traded badly enough to embarrass myself, and the market happened to be generous about it." The more interesting story — and the one I actually want to remember — is what happened at 11:02, when I looked at a third setup, recognized I was done, and didn't touch it.

## OFAL: a reverse split, a distracted miss, and a trade that actually paid the day's bills

<span class="pill pill-hold">1.57M Float</span> <span class="pill pill-slate">Engineering & Construction</span>

OFA Group is an architecture, engineering, and AI-driven Building Information Modeling company that had just completed a **1-for-10 reverse split** on July 31st to cure a Nasdaq bid-price deficiency — the exact mechanical setup for the kind of low-float momentum move that followed. Financial press covering the session called it plainly: a low-float momentum move rather than a fresh fundamental catalyst, the classic signature of a post-split float squeeze.

The move itself: a **289% initial impulse**, a $2.17 dollar move off a **$0.75 base**, then a **42.47% retracement** off that impulse high, a double tap of the 9-EMA on the 15-minute chart, and a fresh surge toward **$3.50**.

{{< callout type="note" title="The good part of the morning" >}}
After the missed setups and a rough stretch trying to catch OFAL consolidating on the 3-minute chart, I finally waited properly — for price to return to the 9-EMA on the 15-minute chart. Entered 50 shares as it touched, added more as it went into a volatility halt near $3. I'd planned to hold toward the 200-day moving average at $6.49, but when OFAL's gap-up-every-5-seconds pattern stopped dead as price approached $3.70, I sold all 100 shares immediately instead of hoping it would resume. That single trade netted roughly **$62** — enough to cover every mistake that came before it on this ticker.

## BIVI: real Parkinson's data, a broken rule, and a fast, correct bailout

<span class="pill pill-hold">7.49M Float</span> <span class="pill pill-slate">Biotech</span>

I didn't check the float before taking this trade. That's worth stating plainly rather than softening — FOMO took over, plain and simple. BioVie Inc. is a clinical-stage biopharmaceutical company, and the real catalyst behind today's move was concrete: its Phase 2 SUNRISE-PD trial for Parkinson's disease met its primary endpoint, improving blood-based biomarkers and reducing associated neuroinflammation. Shares reached a fresh 52-week high near **$3.79**, up over 22% on the day, according to real-time financial coverage.

<div class="tree">
<div class="tree-node decision">10:04 AM — first BIVI trade</div>
<div class="tree-node bad">Broke my own rule: multi-bounce 9-EMA (1-min) setups only count if the impulse leg exceeds $5</div>
<div class="tree-arrow">↓ result</div>

The real opportunity was the double tap of VWAP at **$2.90**, which led to a move up to **$3.80** — clearing the confluence of the 200-day moving average and the 52-week high at **$3.20**. I didn't take it.

At **11:02 AM**, I took a second BIVI trade — 100 shares, right after a strong VWAP sell-off and reclaim, hoping for a surge. I was ready to bail at the first sign of trouble, and I did: price dipped just **8 cents**, from $3.29 to $3.21, and I was out.

{{< callout type="tip" title="A quiet win inside a messy trade" >}}
Getting stopped out over 8 cents sounds like nothing to write about, but it's actually the clearest example of good process in the whole session. I didn't wait to see if it would come back. I didn't average down. The thesis needed an immediate surge; it didn't get one; I left. That's the mechanical exit I've been trying to build all week.
{{< /callout >}}

## BOXL: the one I let go, on purpose

I already had the **100-day moving average marked at $6.85** as a possible entry level. Price consolidated in a tight $6.17–$6.70 range on the 5-minute chart directly beneath it, and then the candle that finally broke that range came in on a disproportionately large volume of roughly **60.21K** — by any definition I use, a textbook buy signal. From a psychological **$5** level, BOXL went on to run **97.79%**, topping out near **$9.90** before selling off.

What I didn't know at the time, and only found out afterward: Boxlight — an education-technology company behind interactive classroom displays — had **two real catalysts** landing the same day. It returned to profitability in its Q2 2026 earnings, beating EPS estimates, and separately closed the initial tranche of a **$5.5 million private placement** alongside a new **$15 million equity line of credit**, both aimed at working capital and debt reduction. The stock had genuinely violent premarket action too — real reporting shows it spiking from roughly $4.35 to above $6.60 within about an hour before settling into the range I was watching. The chart wasn't lying. There was real fuel behind it.

{{< callout type="important" title="The trade I'm choosing not to regret" >}}
I discarded BOXL because there was no major higher-timeframe breakout to point to yet, and by the time the setup was clean, I was mentally fatigued from a morning of fighting my own impulses. I'm genuinely not upset about this one. A tired trader looking at a "perfect" setup is exactly the situation that turns a good day into a bad week — and I'd already spent real effort protecting a hard-earned $53 from a losing streak that had run since last week. Missing a 97.79% run stings less than it would if I'd chased it, gotten sloppy, and given the whole week's progress back in one impulsive trade. Discipline doesn't always look like taking the trade. Sometimes it looks like correctly recognizing you're no longer the trader who should be taking it.
{{< /callout >}}

## What today actually proves

Twenty-two fills is overtrading by any definition, and it's a habit that's clearly getting worse, not better — I'm not going to dress that up. But the session also contained the two things that actually matter more than fill count: a mechanical, unemotional exit on BIVI over eight cents, and the self-awareness to recognize fatigue before it turned into a real mistake on BOXL. Spotting the big movers has never been my problem. Today's real lesson is that stopping — cleanly, before the tank hits empty — is its own skill, separate from entries and exits, and it's the one I finally practiced correctly.
