Trades 4 and 5 had no technical reasoning behind them at all — just an attempt to end the day green after a long stretch of not seeing one.

The setup didn't need a news story to work. My own conviction needed one, apparently, even when my rules didn't.

## Why Trade 1 was actually a good trade

{{< callout type="tip" title="The full confluence, before I ever clicked buy" >}}
- Momentum emerging from the **9-EMA on the hourly chart**
- Strong green volume on the way up, **noticeably lower volume on retracements** — the classic signature of a stock building toward a bigger swing, not just spiking randomly
- Price **double-tapped the 9-EMA on the 1-minute chart** while sitting close to VWAP at the same moment — two confirmations landing together
- Broke the **pre-market high around $5** — a major psychological level — on high volume
- Retraced back to $5 on **reduced volume**, then curled back up — exactly the kind of low-volume pullback that suggests sellers were exhausted, not in control
{{< /callout >}}

Every one of those five conditions was real and present before I entered. This wasn't a lucky guess dressed up afterward as a good read — it was a correctly identified, multi-confirmation setup that I then failed to give room to work.

## What happened after I stopped watching

I closed trading for the day at 10:37 AM. Real trading data for the session shows IMCC still active well into the afternoon, up **144.83%** and trading at **$4.26** as of 3:29 PM — but that's meaningfully below the $7.52 level my third trade was entered at, which tells its own story: the stock ran further after I stepped away, then gave back a real chunk of the move by afternoon. That's worth being honest about rather than only citing the number that supports "I should have held longer." The $350 estimate on Trade 1 assumes an exit somewhere near the late-morning highs, not a hold all the way into the afternoon fade — the lesson isn't just "let winners run," it's "let winners run **and still have a plan for where you get out.**"

## The two rules I keep re-learning

{{< callout type="important" title="On staying in a trade" >}}
I've genuinely gotten better at cutting losses and controlling risk — Trade 2's 8-second exit is real proof of that. What I haven't fixed is the opposite skill: staying committed to a winning trade long enough to let the read pay off. Watching P&L tick in real time is what breaks this. P&L is the *outcome* of the process, not the process itself, and reacting to the number instead of the setup is exactly how a 19-second trade happens on a stock that kept running for hours afterward.
{{< /callout >}}

{{< callout type="note" title="On days without much happening" >}}
Trends on days with multiple big movers tend to last longer than trends on quiet days — today had just the one. That's part of why I stopped at 10:37 rather than keep hunting. It was also Friday, and [Monday through Wednesday has historically produced the biggest moves](/posts/2026-09-14-elmt-ftft/) in my own tracked data — one more reason a quiet Friday afternoon wasn't worth forcing.
{{< /callout >}}

The harder lesson is the one about switching tickers. I have a real tendency to hop from stock to stock hunting for the next mover, and I'm only recently internalizing that when there are no good trades, there are no good trades — chasing motion for its own sake is exactly how trades 4 and 5 happened. Those two weren't a read on IMCC. They were an attempt to erase Trade 2's damage and end the day positive, since it had been a long stretch since the last genuinely green one. That's revenge trading by definition, and it cost the small edge Trade 3 had already clawed back.

## What today actually proves

I don't have a stock-picking problem. Five trades on one ticker, one of which was built on a real, five-part technical confluence that played out exactly as read — the analysis isn't the gap. The gap is narrow and specific: mechanical execution once the read is already correct. Trade 1 needed patience it didn't get. Trades 4 and 5 needed a reason to exist that they never had. Both failures point at the same fix, and it isn't a new indicator or a better scanner — it's the discipline to let a correct read run its course without needing the P&L ticker to confirm it every second along the way.
